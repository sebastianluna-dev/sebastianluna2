<CaseSection id="overview" title="A string quartet, from first listen to booking">

Cuarteto Fratres is a string quartet from Boca del Río, Veracruz, playing chamber music in concert halls, ceremonies and private events. I designed and built their website to showcase their music and make booking them easy.

It is a single page that takes visitors from discovering the quartet to getting in touch: a hero with the upcoming dates, an introduction to the group, profiles of the four musicians, the repertoire with its player and a contact form.

I handled the project end to end: product, design, development, CMS modeling and deployment. Everything a visitor sees is edited in Payload CMS, which lives inside the same Next.js app, so the group updates its own content and handles its own booking requests.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="challenge" title="Two audiences on one page">

The site had to work for someone planning a concert, a wedding or a private event. They need to know when the quartet plays, who is in it, what they play and how to ask for a proposal, all without leaving the page.

On the other side is the group, who needed to publish their dates, adjust their repertoire and answer requests without me touching the code.

### Dates come first

That is why the hero opens with the next two published performances. Each card opens a window with the date’s details: description, program, venue, prices and a tickets link. When a date has no ticket sales, the button goes straight to the contact form.

<CaseSection id="approach" title="One well-ordered page and a CMS inside the app">

I laid the experience out as a single page so the journey runs from meeting the quartet to contacting them. Every section has its own anchor and the fixed header follows the whole way: it is transparent over the dark hero photo and turns ivory once the light sections begin. On phones, a full-screen menu numbers the four sections.

### A CMS inside the site

Payload lives inside the Next.js app, at /admin, on PostgreSQL. Each section’s copy is a Global and whatever repeats is a collection: members, performances, repertoire, categories and images. Components never read Payload directly: each section asks a service for its data, which maps the document to a type of its own and falls back, field by field, to the launch content when something is empty, so the page is never blank.

<CaseSection id="research" title="Content organized by occasion">

I organized the content around the occasions the quartet plays for: concerts and cultural events, weddings and ceremonies, and private events. The repertoire is grouped into categories such as Chamber, Latin American, Weddings and Pop, so each visitor quickly finds what suits their event.

The CMS takes up to five categories, as many as fit in the filter on a phone, and will not delete one that still has pieces in it. Either way it tells the editor why, instead of letting the page break quietly. The form asks for just three things, and the section copy invites people to include the date, the venue and the expected length, which is what the group needs to put together a program proposal.

<CaseSection id="design" title="A player drawn as a score">

There are no studio recordings yet: the player simulates playback over each piece’s length, and only one hook changes once the audio exists. The interface is already complete.

The repertoire is the centerpiece. The selected piece lives on a dark card with a lime treble clef as its cover; when the title does not fit, it scrolls sideways like a car stereo. The progress bar is a staff drawn twice, faint underneath and bright on top, with the bright copy clipped to the part already played, so the notes light up as the piece moves along. A double bar line marks the position, and a click jumps to any point.

### Nothing starts on its own

Visitors decide when to press play, and only one piece is in progress at a time. If the card scrolls off screen during playback, a mini player pins itself to the bottom so people can keep reading without losing control. It leaves as soon as the card is back in view, or five seconds after a pause, and while it is there the footer adds its height as padding so nothing gets covered. On phones there is no card: the bar is the whole player, with a progress line along its bottom edge.

<CaseSubsection kicker="Members">

The four musicians appear in a gallery of portrait strips. One is always open; choosing another widens it and swaps the card beside it, with instrument, name and biography. Strips open on click rather than hover, so they don’t open just because the pointer crosses them.

</CaseSubsection>

<CaseSubsection kicker="Requests">

The form posts to a server action, so it works even before the page loads its JavaScript. Validation reports every error at once and caps the lengths on the server too. Every valid request is stored in the CMS as “New”; the team marks it “Answered” or “Discarded” and leaves internal notes, while the sender’s details stay read-only. The public API cannot create requests: the server action is the only way in.

<CaseSection id="outcome" title="A site the group runs on its own">

The quartet has a site that introduces them and takes their booking requests, and the group publishes dates, adjusts its repertoire and handles every request from /admin. On save, hooks invalidate the cache for that data and for the home page, so the change shows up on the next visit.

I left the project ready to grow: the database schema only changes through migrations, any logic that can be tested without React lives in pure functions with a test beside them, and continuous integration checks style and types, runs the tests and builds the site on every push. Sections fade in as they enter the screen, and visitors who prefer less motion see them in full from the start.
