<CaseSection id="overview" title="A string quartet with no place online">

Cuarteto Fratres is a string quartet from Boca del Río, Veracruz. On stage it was already known. Online it did not exist: no mark, no page, nothing to find when someone searched its name.

I had been to many of their concerts before I approached them with a proposal: build the brand and give it a home. They hired me for all of it. I drew the monogram, designed a display typeface, set the palette, took the photographs, and designed and built the site and the CMS behind it. The site scores 98 for performance and 100 for accessibility, best practices and SEO on Lighthouse mobile, and 126 tests cover its logic.

Its players lead sections in the region's orchestras: Jesús Medina is concertmaster of the Orquesta Filarmónica de Boca del Río, Alfonso Pérez is its principal cellist, José Sánchez is principal violist of the state youth symphony, and Lucía Paredes, who founded the quartet, plays in the Filarmónica's violins.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="challenge" title="Two audiences, one tone">

I designed the site for people booking performances and for the quartet members who update it. The first are planning a concert, a wedding or a private event and need to know who plays, what they play and how to ask for a proposal. The second have to publish dates, change the repertoire and answer requests without calling me.

The visual tone matters to both audiences. Chamber music has a printed culture of its own: programmes, posters, scores. To my eye, most musicians' sites ignore it and end up looking like a template for wedding services. I set myself the opposite brief: the site should feel like sitting in the hall a minute before the concert starts.

### The hall with the lights down

That image decided the palette. The ground is a near black, the hall in the dark, so that the only colour on screen comes from skin and from the instruments. The warm tones around it, cream and ivory, are wood and old score paper. Nothing else was added: I tried an accent colour and a dark brown during the build and removed both.

<CaseSection id="approach" title="A brand before a website">

The mark is a CF monogram that I drew in Illustrator. It holds at the size of a browser tab and at the size of a poster, and it is the one element that appears on every page.

### A typeface with a job

I wanted a display face I could push around the way poster designers do: tall, condensed capitals that hold a line on their own. Fratres Display is capitals only. I started it with ChatGPT, generating letterforms to react to, and then redrew and corrected them by hand in Illustrator until the set did what I needed. It does not derive from an existing font.

I limit Fratres Display to specific uses on the site. It sets the quartet's name in the hero, each musician's name on their own page and the posters of the bill, where one line has to carry the screen. Section titles use Italianno, a classical script that can repeat down the page without taking attention from what follows. A typeface made for posters has to know when to stay quiet on a website.

I took the photographs too, some on an iPhone 17 Pro Max and some on a Canon EOS 90D, and removed the background of several portraits with AI tools so the musicians could stand directly on the dark ground.

<CaseSubsection kicker="Visual direction" appear>

The hero is the hall. Four musicians stand on black, lit, facing you. Behind them run staff lines, and on the lines there is music.

It is real music: bars of Borodin's String Quartet No. 2, which the quartet performs, and of Pachelbel's Canon. I wrote an engraver that places every note on its line or space along curved staves, so the notation stays correct while the lines sway, and thins the ink wherever a face is.

<CaseSection id="research" title="How I made decisions">

I used AI agents, mainly Claude Code, to build this project. The agent writes code, proposes and measures. I direct, and I decide.

For every visual decision I asked for several distinct directions, never one: icon styles, hero backgrounds, the event window. I looked at them rendered in the browser at desktop and phone widths, picked one or mixed two, and discarded the rest.

Some of what I discarded I had asked for myself. I had the repertoire section built on a wood texture, in several versions, and reverted all of it because it competed with the photographs. I tried setting the section titles in Fratres Display, disliked it within a minute, and went back. Seeing quickly that something is wrong is most of the job.

Nothing stayed without being checked. Each change was built, captured at 1440 and 390 pixels wide and committed on its own, and the logic lives in pure functions with their tests beside them.

<CaseSection id="design" title="Pieces that behave like their objects">

The repertoire, events and contact sections take their cues from a record, a printed programme and a letter.

<CaseSubsection kicker="A record and a track list">

The repertoire is a record player. The work that is playing turns on a disc with the quartet's photograph as its label. The list beside it is numbered, and each row shows its number, a play glyph under the pointer, or moving level bars while it sounds, the way streaming apps have taught everyone to read. Filters sort the works by occasion: chamber, Latin American, weddings, pop. If you scroll away while music plays, the disc follows in a corner of the screen. The player is built to hold the quartet's own recordings.

<CaseSection id="outcome" title="A brand the quartet runs itself">

Cuarteto Fratres now has a name you can search, a mark, a typeface, photographs and a site at cuartetofratres.com. Everything a visitor reads is edited in a CMS that lives inside the same application, so the musicians publish dates, reorder the repertoire and triage requests on their own. When there is no upcoming date, the bill removes itself from the page.

<CaseSubsection kicker="Engineering">

On a throttled phone connection the largest element painted in 2.6 seconds; I brought it to 1.3. The audit pointed elsewhere; I found a late-loading display font and duplicated inline music: the display font was the last file to arrive, and the engraved music was inlined twice in the page, 375 KB of it. Preloading the right two fonts and serving the music as static files took the page from 463 KB to 139 KB. I also tried inlining the CSS, measured that it made things worse, and removed it. Lighthouse on mobile reads 98 for performance and 100 for accessibility, best practices and SEO, and 126 tests cover the logic.
