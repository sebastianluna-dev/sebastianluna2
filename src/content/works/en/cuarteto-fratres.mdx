<CaseSection id="overview" title="The quartet and the brief">

Cuarteto Fratres is a string quartet from Boca del Río, Veracruz. On stage it was already known. Online it did not exist: no mark, no page, nothing to find when someone searched its name.

I had been to many of their concerts before I approached them with a proposal: build the brand and give it a home. They hired me for all of it. I drew the monogram, designed a display typeface, set the palette, took the photographs, and designed and built the site and the CMS behind it. The site scores 98 for performance and 100 for accessibility, best practices and SEO on Lighthouse mobile, and 126 tests cover its logic.

The four musicians also play in the region's orchestras: Jesús Medina is concertmaster of the Boca del Río Philharmonic Orchestra, Alfonso Pérez is its principal cellist, José Sánchez is principal violist of the state youth symphony, and Lucía Paredes, who founded the quartet, plays in the Philharmonic's violins.

<CaseSection id="challenge" title="Two jobs for one site">

I designed the site for people booking performances and for the quartet members who update it. The first are planning a concert, a wedding or a private event and need to know who plays, what they play and how to ask for a proposal. The second have to publish dates, change the repertoire and answer requests without calling me.

The visual tone matters to both audiences. Chamber music has a printed culture of its own: programmes, posters, scores. I did not want the site to look like a template for wedding services. I wanted it to feel like sitting in the hall a minute before the concert starts.

### The hall with the lights down

That image decided the palette. The ground is a near black, the hall in the dark, so that the only colour on screen comes from skin and from the instruments. The warm tones around it, cream and ivory, are wood and old score paper. Nothing else was added: I tried an accent colour and a dark brown during the build and removed both.

<CaseSection id="approach" title="Identity, type and photography">

The mark is a CF monogram that I drew in Illustrator. It holds at the size of a browser tab and at the size of a poster, and it is the one element that appears on every page.

### A typeface with a job

I wanted a display face I could push around the way poster designers do: tall, condensed capitals that hold a line on their own. Fratres Display is capitals only. I started it with ChatGPT, generating letterforms to react to, and then redrew and corrected them by hand in Illustrator until the set did what I needed. It does not derive from an existing font.

I limit Fratres Display to specific uses on the site. It sets the quartet's name in the hero, each musician's name on their own page and the posters of the bill, where one line has to carry the screen. Section titles use Italianno, a classical script that can repeat down the page without taking attention from what follows.

I took the photographs too, some on an iPhone 17 Pro Max and some on a Canon EOS 90D, and removed the background of several portraits with AI tools so the musicians could stand directly on the dark ground.

<CaseSubsection kicker="Visual direction" appear>

The hero is the hall. Four musicians stand on black, lit, facing you. Behind them run staff lines, and on the lines there is music.

It is real music: bars of Borodin's String Quartet No. 2, which the quartet performs, and of Pachelbel's Canon. I wrote an engraver that places every note on its line or space along curved staves, so the notation stays correct while the lines sway, and thins the ink wherever a face is.

<CaseSection id="research" title="AI-assisted build and review">

I designed how the site looks and behaves, from the darkened hall and the staves behind the musicians to the spinning record, the printed programme and the letter. I built it with AI agents, mainly Claude Code, which wrote the code and measured the results.

When I had several directions to consider, I asked the agents to build them side by side: different icon styles, hero backgrounds or versions of the event window. I compared them in the browser at desktop and phone widths. If none felt right, I designed the piece myself, down to its position, size, spacing and type, then worked through the changes until the screen matched.

Every change went through a build, screenshots at 1440 and 390 px and a separate commit. The logic is organized into pure functions, with tests alongside them.

<CaseSection id="design" title="Pieces that behave like their objects">

The repertoire, events and contact sections take their cues from a record, a printed programme and a letter.

<CaseSubsection kicker="A record and a track list">

The repertoire is a record player. The work that is playing turns on a disc with the quartet's photograph as its label. The list beside it is numbered, and each row shows its number, a play glyph under the pointer, or moving level bars while it sounds, the way streaming apps have taught everyone to read. Filters group the works: chamber, Latin American, weddings, pop. If you scroll away while music plays, the disc follows in a corner of the screen. The player is built to hold the quartet's own recordings.

<CaseSection id="outcome" title="A brand the quartet runs itself">

Cuarteto Fratres now has a name you can search, a mark, a typeface, photographs and a site at cuartetofratres.com. Everything a visitor reads is edited in a CMS that lives inside the same application, so the musicians publish dates, reorder the repertoire and triage requests on their own. When there is no upcoming date, the bill removes itself from the page.

<CaseSubsection kicker="Engineering">

On a throttled phone connection the largest element painted in 2.6 seconds; I brought it to 1.3. The audit pointed elsewhere; I found a late-loading display font and duplicated inline music: the display font was the last file to arrive, and the engraved music was inlined twice in the page, 375 KB of it. Preloading the right two fonts and serving the music as static files took the page from 463 KB to 139 KB. I also tried inlining the CSS, measured that it made things worse, and removed it.
