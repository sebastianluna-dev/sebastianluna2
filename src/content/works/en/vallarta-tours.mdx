<CaseSection id="overview" title="A site to pick a trip and book it in two languages">

Vallarta TOURS is the fictional brand I use to show the site I designed and built for a boat tour operator in Puerto Vallarta. The original site was live and has been taken offline; this sample version keeps the work under a fictional brand, and its prices, reviews, contact details and legal texts are filler. The sample presents five trips on Banderas Bay: Arcos – Ánimas – Quimixto, Yelapa – Majahuitas, the Marietas Islands, whale watching and a private charter.

The project started at Proficient Systems. I ran the research with the client, drew the wireframes and the high-fidelity designs in Figma, helped define the site’s voice and experience, and built the frontend, with bookings, payments and availability integrated through Shopify. The version shown here is one I rebuilt in 2025 with Next.js, React, TypeScript and next-intl: six routes in Spanish and English, with translated URLs.

<CaseSection id="challenge" title="Five trips, each with its own rules">

No two trips are alike. In the sample, each one has its own price per person, length, departure times, group size per boat and dock, in Puerto Vallarta or Nuevo Vallarta. The whales only run from December to March, and the private charter has no fixed price and no itinerary at fixed times: it is quoted.

All of that had to read just as clearly in Spanish as in English, page addresses included, and take visitors from an idea to a booking without extra steps.

### From the home page to the booking

The home page bar asks for the experience, the date and the group size. The search lands on that trip’s page, where the booking card is already filled in with what was chosen above.

<CaseSection id="approach" title="Content first, pages second">

I split the content into two places. Everything a visitor reads lives in two catalogs, one per language, with the same keys; Spanish is the reference catalog and defines the type of every string the code uses. What does not change with the language, such as prices, departure times, group sizes, docks and seasons, lives in constants.

Adding a trip means one entry in the master list and its block in both catalogs. The detail pages, the sitemap, the services panels and the carousel all come from that same list.

### Translated URLs

The routes are translated too: `/servicios` is `/en/services`, and a trip with a descriptive name changes its address, like `avistamiento-de-ballenas` and `whale-watching`. Each language answers only to its own, the language switch keeps you on the same page and, on a first visit, the site sends you to English if your browser prefers it.

<CaseSection id="research" title="What you need to know before boarding">

On the original project I started with research alongside the client, and moved from there to wireframes and designs in Figma. In the sample, every detail page answers what you need before booking: length, price, departure times, group size, departure dock, what is included, what is not, what to bring and the FAQ.

### Minding every line

The “what to bring” labels never run past 20 characters, which is what fits on one line of their pill, and no trip asks for more than six things. When a trip’s full name would take three lines in the large headline, the page uses a shorter version.

<CaseSubsection kicker="Custom fields">

The date and the group size are picked with the same two fields in the home page bar and in the card of each trip. I dropped the native list and date picker, because every browser draws them its own way: the date is a month of React Day Picker that opens over the page, and the group size is a Radix list. From Radix I took the behavior (keyboard, focus and a layer that is not clipped inside the hero); the design is mine.

<CaseSubsection kicker="Booking card">

You pick a departure time, a date and a group size, and the card adds up the total and works out the 30% deposit. “Book” opens WhatsApp with the trip, the date, the time and the number of people already written.

The charter has three departure times and no fixed time for its stops: they are stored as minutes from departure, so the itinerary shows the real time of the departure you pick in the card. Both sections share that time.

<CaseSubsection kicker="Trip carousel">

One piece of markup serves both layouts. On desktop, the photo of the active trip fills the stage and the next ones stack diagonally; each trip is drawn three times so the deck always moves to the left, and no card ever slides back across the others. On phones the cards fan out and follow your finger. While half the section is on screen, the arrow keys move the deck, and only two of the five large photos load at first; the rest arrive as you go.

<CaseSection id="outcome" title="Six routes in two languages, served static">

The site has six routes in each language (ten pages, with the detail of each trip): home, services, the detail of each trip, contact, terms and the privacy notice. The content lives in the repository and everything is served static. The same project generates the sitemap with the pages of both languages linked to each other, the `robots.txt` and a social card per language.

Any logic that can be tested without React is a pure function with its test beside it: prices, times, months, seasons, totals, WhatsApp and email messages, and the carousel’s moves. That makes 67 tests in 17 files, and continuous integration checks style and types, runs the tests and builds the site on every change. It is deployed on Vercel.
